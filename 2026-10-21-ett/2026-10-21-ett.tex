\begin{frame}{Cities hold long data, not big data}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Big data is wide and shallow: many records, short history.
  \item Municipal data is narrow and deep: one jurisdiction, continuous,
        across generations.
  \item Boulder incorporated November 4, 1871. Home rule charter adopted
        October 30, 1917, amended ever since.
\end{itemize}

\vfill
\muted{The term is Samuel Arbesman's, from a 2013 essay arguing that
datasets spanning decades show what no snapshot can.}

\vfill
\alert{Duration is the asset. Nothing else the city owns runs 154 years
deep.}

\note{Land the reframe before anything technical. Everyone in the room
has heard a big-data pitch. Nobody has heard this one.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 3
\begin{frame}{Why that matters now}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Most of what gets pitched is built for big data --- scale,
        throughput, statistical power.
  \item Retrieval, knowledge graphs, and agent harnesses fit long data
        better: small corpus, high provenance requirement, entity
        resolution across time.
  \item Document intelligence changed the price of reading. An accurate
        machine reading of a scanned page now costs under a cent.
\end{itemize}

\vfill
\begin{block}{The claim}
The binding requirement here is provenance: find the 1974 staff
memorandum and say which page it is on.
\end{block}

\vfill
\muted{What stays expensive is knowing where to look and checking what
the machine read. That is where infrastructure pays.}

\note{The price-of-reading bullet is the hinge for the middle of the
talk. The sprints on slide 7 are the evidence for it.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 4
\begin{frame}{Long data has three failure modes. None is about secrecy.}

\begin{description}
  \item[Erosion.] The record degrades through ordinary operations. The
    hotline archive splits at a 2023 server migration. Attachments were
    severed from the messages that cite them. OpenColorado, the shared
    repository for Colorado jurisdictions, has been retired.
  \item[Enclosure.] Documents stay public while the routes to them are
    fenced: opaque numeric identifiers, automated access disallowed,
    export ceilings, custody split across four separate custodians.
  \item[Exemption.] What never enters the record at all. Deliberation
    that moves to a phone call. Material never digitized, which is
    functionally nonexistent.
\end{description}

\vfill
\muted{Countered, respectively, by ownership, openness, and oversight.}

\note{These three are the analytic for the whole talk and for whatever
the task force looks at next. Do not rush them.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 5
\begin{frame}{One artifact, both forces}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The Charter of the City of Boulder, adopted October 30, 1917,
        sits in our document portal.
  \item That portal disallows automated access and addresses documents
        by opaque integers.
  \item The same charter is a free download from the university law
        library --- item 31 in a collection of historical Colorado
        municipal codes.
\end{itemize}

\vfill
\alert{We digitized it. They made it downloadable.}

\vfill
\muted{Erosion and enclosure in a single object, and the opening of the
argument for partnership.}

\note{Let the last line sit before moving on. Frame it as an offer:
the law library is a partner we already have, and they have shown what
the work looks like.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{We already tried it: two liberation sprints}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\begin{block}{City budget books, 2002--2027}
\small
34 PDFs and 10,843 pages from the city's own document portal. Six
volumes, 1,651 pages, have no text layer at all. The budget portal
offers no public data endpoint.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Result:} 450 figures across 26 years, each traced to its
source document.
\end{block}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\begin{block}{State school yearbooks, 1986--1999}
\small
The scans' built-in text layer recovered 3.9\% of one table's cells.
Accurate OCR read the rest at 0.75 cents a page, about \$5 for
fourteen volumes.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Result:} 1,267 of 1,269 figures read twice agree exactly.
\end{block}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\vfill
\alert{Reading the pages cost dollars. Everything after --- finding
the right pages, checking every figure, naming every gap --- was the
work.}

\vfill
\muted{\footnotesize Source: Charting Boulder repository, budget-history
and alternatives folders.}

\note{First person here: I did this on my own time for a newspaper
column. If one person can do it for a column, the city can do it for
the public record.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 8
\begin{frame}{Stewardship before retrieval}

\begin{block}{Archive layer}
\small Originals exactly as received and never edited. Each carries a
checksum, its source URL, and its retrieval date.
\end{block}
\begin{block}{Derived layer}
\small Text and tables extracted from the originals. Every value names
its source document, machine readings are marked as such, each
document's totals are checked against its own parts, and a failed check
stops the build. Gaps are named, never filled.
\end{block}
\begin{block}{Retrieval layer}
\small Search, pipelines, and agents. Every answer cites back down to a
document and page.
\end{block}

\vfill
\muted{\small Each rule answers a failure. OCR invented values from
percentages and read a bar chart as numbers; the book's own arithmetic
caught both. A pipeline once wrote nine copies of fourteen years of
records, and every total still looked right.}

\note{This is the argument that the data lake needs a spine. Retrieval
without the two layers beneath it is a fluent guess.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 10
\begin{frame}{Obligation: accessibility remediation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Scanned image documents are unreadable to screen readers.
  \item Six volumes of the city's own budget books, 1,651 pages, have no
        text layer at all.
  \item Federal accessibility obligations make the text layer owed
        regardless of whether we build anything on top of it.
\end{itemize}

\vfill
\begin{block}{The budget argument}
The spending is already owed. The choice is whether it produces a
reusable public corpus or a pile of remediated documents.
\end{block}

\note{Lead with this in any conversation involving money. It is the only
commitment with an external deadline attached.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 11
\begin{frame}{Obligation: release from vendor lock-in}

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
  \item \textbf{Stable, resolvable identifiers for every document.}
        Unglamorous, cheap, and the precondition for everything after.
  \item \textbf{Bulk export and programmatic access as contract terms}
        at renewal. Twenty-six years of budget history had to be rebuilt
        from PDFs because the budget portal offers no data endpoint.
  \item \textbf{Open document standards} for legislative markup, scanned
        images, and preservation metadata. This makes vendors
        interchangeable without requiring us to win an argument about
        any particular one.
  \item \textbf{Extend the licensing posture we already have.}
\end{enumerate}

\vfill
\muted{The open data catalog is already published under a license
permitting copying, adaptation, and redistribution without attribution.
The practice exists. It stops at the portal's edge.}

\note{Item 4 is the disarming one: we are proposing consistency with our
own practice, not a new philosophy.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 12
\begin{frame}{Opportunity: community innovation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The open data program already partners with local civic data
        groups.
  \item A local civic-tech group already maintains a directory of
        roughly a hundred Boulder datasets.
  \item The law library already digitized our charter.
  \item I rebuilt 26 years of the city's budget history from its own
        PDFs for a newspaper column.
\end{itemize}

\vfill
\alert{A published corpus becomes a data lake that staff, partners,
pipelines, and agents can all draw from.}

\vfill
\muted{The constraint is staff time. Publishing a corpus converts
outside interest into capacity the city never has to hire for, and it is
the only one of these commitments that pays back without a procurement.}

\note{Name the groups if the room knows them. This is also the answer to
``we don't have the staff for this.''}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 13
\begin{frame}{What this looks like elsewhere}

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
  \item National legislative databases publish point-in-time versions of
        law, so a reader can see what was in force on a given date.
  \item Federal programs publish legislative data as bulk downloads
        rather than only through a search box.
  \item National archives run text and handwriting recognition across
        historical holdings at scale.
  \item Heritage institutions use volunteer transcription as a capacity
        model requiring no procurement.
  \item Court systems publish structured opinions with stable citation.
  \item Newsrooms build document-mining pipelines for large records
        releases.
\end{itemize}

\vfill
\muted{Six sectors, one pattern: publish the corpus, treat the interface
as replaceable.}

\note{If someone asks which vendor does this: the question is the trap.
Every example here is an institution or a sector.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 14
\begin{frame}{What happens next, and why I might be wrong}

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
  \item Context windows may grow enough that retrieval architecture
        becomes unnecessary. The provenance requirement survives either
        way, which is why I anchored on it.
  \item The binding constraint has already moved from compute to
        verification and permission: retention schedules, legal review,
        metadata quality. None of those follow a hardware curve.
  \item Multi-step agentic research on legal precedent is unmeasured on
        municipal corpora. Errors compound and nothing surfaces them.
  \item \textbf{Demand may be low.} The hotline suggests otherwise, but
        one coder and forty messages is thin evidence.
  \item The most likely failure is not technical. A multi-year project
        dies on a budget cycle and one departure.
\end{itemize}

\note{Say the demand bullet flat. It is the strongest objection to my own
recommendation and the room should hear me make it first.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 15
\begin{frame}{Three varieties of information}

\begin{description}
  \item[Already data.] Published, licensed, documented. The catalog.
  \item[Could become data.] The archives, the budget books, the hotline
    and its attachments, the recordings, the backlog. Where the
    technology argument lives.
  \item[Shouldn't become data.] The category with no policy, because
    nobody writes one until the corpus already exists.
\end{description}

\vfill
\alert{Sorting a holding into one of these three is a decision the city
can make deliberately. Not sorting is also a decision.}

\note{This is the deliverable. Offer to circulate this slide alone.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 16
\begin{frame}{Shouldn't become data}

Candidates: surveillance feeds, individual utility and recreation
customer records, code enforcement complaints naming residents,
protective-order addresses, records carrying victim and juvenile
information.

\vfill
\begin{itemize}
  \item The argument is about \textbf{aggregation, not disclosure}.
        Records obligations do not change. What changes is whether the
        city builds the index that makes assembling a dossier trivial.
  \item Practical obscurity is a protection nobody wrote down. Retrieval
        removes it unless someone decides to keep it.
  \item Precedent exists: the published hotline dataset already replaces
        contact details with one-way hashes.
\end{itemize}

\vfill
\begin{block}{The inversion}
Exemption is the force to resist in the first two categories and the
mechanism to use in the third. Scoped properly, it is what makes the
other two safe to build.
\end{block}

\note{Guard against being heard as anti-transparency. Say the
aggregation/disclosure distinction twice if needed.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 17
\begin{frame}{Would impacts fall unevenly in our community?}

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{4pt}
  \item \textbf{Digitization order becomes citation order.} What is not
        scanned is functionally nonexistent to a researcher or an
        analyst, and the least-digitized holdings are disproportionately
        those of the least-powerful.
  \item \textbf{Text recognition fails unevenly.} It fails worst on old,
        handwritten, tabular, and non-English material, so retrieval
        quality degrades along the same axis as the equity concern.
  \item \textbf{Aggregation lands unevenly.} It falls hardest on
        residents with the most contact with enforcement systems.
\end{enumerate}

\vfill
\alert{It lands on the residents least able to object. It lands through
records the city already holds. It lands whether or not anyone intended
it.}

\note{Do not soften this into a caveat. It is a sequencing constraint:
it should change which holdings we digitize first.}
\end{frame}

%----------------------------------------------------------------------- 18
\begin{frame}{Where this leaves us}

Ten minutes in a browser and two sprints are not an audit. The finding I
hold with the most confidence is the one we cannot close from in here:
we do not know the size of our own backlog.

\vfill
The test arrives with the first proposal that comes to us with a
demonstration attached. If we cannot say which of the three categories
its corpus belongs to, or which layer of the stack it is selling, the
demonstration is doing the sorting for us.

\vfill
\alert{What the city still holds after a vendor leaves is a choice
available now, while it is cheap.}

\note{End on the choice. The sorting rule and the stack are the handout;
everything before them was the argument for taking them seriously.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Appendix: hotline method}
\small
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
  \item \textbf{Source.} City open data layer ``Emails to the City Council
        Hotline'' (CC0): 885 records, March 2, 2023 to September 15, 2026.
        106 are off-list or unsolicited mail; 779 remain.
  \item \textbf{Text.} Subject plus the authored top of each message.

  \item \textbf{Themes.} Nine keyword dictionaries, multi-label, on all

  \item \textbf{Checks.} Primary theme matched in about 22 of 25 messages

        provocation slide is a separate hand-coded sample of 40.
  \item \textbf{Limits.} Keywords see words, not intent. Attachments,
        where many answers live, are not in the data.
\end{itemize}
