\begin{frame}{Long data}
    \textit{Big data} means many records but typically over a short span

    \medskip

    Municipal records often run continuously for decades $\rightarrow$ \textit{long data}

    \medskip

    Long data can illustrate trends, establish precedents, enable comparisons, and uncover context 

    \medskip

    Long data also poses challenges around accessibility, standards, comparability

\begin{frame}{Document understanding}
    \begin{columns}
        \begin{column}{width=.65\textwidth}
            \textit{Document understanding} is a research field and software frameworks that turn documents into data

            \medskip
            
            \begin{itemize}\small
                \item \textbf{Layout analysis} finds the headings, paragraphs, tables, and figures on a page, and the order to read them in
                \item \textbf{Optical character recognition (OCR)} reads printed or handwritten text from a picture of a page
                \item \textbf{Table recognition} rebuilds a table's rows, columns, and cells
                \item \textbf{Field extraction} pulls named items, such as a date or a total, out of a form
            \end{itemize}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{width=.3\textwidth}
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{2026-10-21-ett/img/datalab.png}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Data liberation and rescue}
    \textit{Data liberation} moves information that is already public out of the formats that bury it (scans, PDFs, portals) and into open and accessible data formats

    \textit{Data rescue} is a grassroots interdisciplinary movement to preserve public datasets from decay and censorship

    \medskip

    \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
        \item \textbf{2014.} The Sunlight Foundation runs a PDF Liberation Hackathon to build shared tools for pulling tables out of government PDFs
        \item \textbf{2017 on.} The Public Utility Data Liberation project turns federal energy filings into an open and documented database
        \item \textbf{2020.} SCOTUS rules in \textit{Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org} that the state's annotated code cannot be copyrighted
        \item \textbf{2025 on.} Researchers improvise technical capacity to archive data before it is deleted by the Trump administration
    \end{itemize}

    \bottomcite{Tom Lee, ``PDF Liberation: Why It Matters and How You Can Help,'' Sunlight Foundation, January 24, 2014; \textit{Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc.}, 590 U.S. 255 (2020)}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What liberation could do for the city}
    \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{4pt}
        \item \textbf{Hotline answers.} Aiding staff in research to answer questions from council and other departments
        \item \textbf{Budget history.} Converting PDF tables into accessible data formats
        \item \textbf{The law on a date.} Which version of an ordinance was in force when a permit was issued
        \item \textbf{Records requests.} A document made searchable once is ready for every later request that asks for it
        \item \textbf{Accessibility.} Every scanned page gains text that a screen reader can speak
        \item \textbf{Research and reporting.} Residents, reporters, and the university work from the same corpus as staff
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Accessibility}
    A scanned page is a picture. A screen reader, the software that reads a page aloud to a blind resident, finds nothing on it

    \medskip

    Six volumes of the city's own budget books, 1,651 pages, are pictures of this kind

    \medskip

    Federal rules under the Americans with Disabilities Act require the city's web content, posted PDFs included, to meet an accessibility standard (WCAG 2.1, level AA) by April 26, 2027. In April 2026 the Justice Department pushed that date back a year

    \medskip

    Colorado's own law has applied since July 2024, and its grace period for good-faith effort ended in July 2025. A resident can now sue for \$3,500 per violation

    \bigskip

    \begin{alertblock}{Owed in part}
        Documents residents use to get a service must be readable. Old scans kept in a marked archive, for reference only, are exempt, so making them readable is the city's choice
    \end{alertblock}

    \bottomcite{28 C.F.R. \S\S 35.200--35.201, as amended by 91 Fed.\ Reg.\ 20902 (April 20, 2026); C.R.S. \S 24-34-802 (HB21-1110, HB24-1454)}

    \note{Taskforce prompt: key opportunities and risks. Lead with this in any conversation about money: it carries an external deadline. The archive exemption has four conditions: made before the deadline, kept only for reference or recordkeeping, unaltered, and in a clearly marked archive. An old PDF loses its exemption if residents use it to apply for or reach a service.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Colorado Open Records Act}
    Colorado declares that ``all public records shall be open for inspection by any person at reasonable times,'' except where the law says otherwise

    \medskip

    A custodian has three working days to respond, or ten in extenuating circumstances. The legislature passed a longer deadline in 2025, and the governor vetoed it

    \medskip

    A record kept in a searchable or sortable format must be released that way. A scan that is only a picture of a page can be released as a picture. The first hour of searching is free; after that the city may charge up to \$41.37 an hour

    \medskip

    Every request sends a person searching. A record liberated once is ready for the next request before anyone files it

    \bottomcite{Colorado Open Records Act, C.R.S. \S\S 24-72-201, 24-72-203(3) and (3.5), 24-72-205(6); SB25-077 (vetoed April 17, 2025)}

    \note{Taskforce prompt: key opportunities. This is the obligation residents use most directly. Liberation doesn't change what is public under CORA; it changes how long finding it takes.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What happens next}
    \begin{columns}
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
            Reading a scanned page costs under a cent today, and the price keeps falling

            \medskip

            Finding the right document, checking what the machine read, and deciding what may be released will still take people. Retention schedules and legal review don't get cheaper with faster chips

            \medskip

            Vendors will arrive with products running on the city's own documents. For the hotline, the product worth buying writes a draft answer with citations, which a staff member checks and signs
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
            \centering

        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \note{Taskforce prompt: what do we think will happen. The meme is the whole recommendation in two panels.}

\begin{frame}{Why I might be wrong}
    \begin{columns}
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
            Demand may be low. My evidence is one coder and 40 hotline answers

            \medskip

            Models may outgrow search. If a model can read every record at once, the city needs less search machinery, though it still needs every answer to cite a page

            \medskip

            An automated assistant that chains several lookups can go wrong at each step without flagging it. Nobody has measured how often on city records

            \medskip

            Most likely of all, the project just stops: multi-year records work dies on one budget cycle or one staff departure
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/this-is-fine.jpg}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \note{Taskforce prompt: why might we be wrong. Say the demand point flatly: it is the strongest objection to my own recommendation, and the room should hear me make it first. The last point is the most likely failure, and it isn't technical.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Some records shouldn't become data}
    \begin{columns}
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
            Sort every holding into one of three kinds: \textbf{already data}, \textbf{could become data}, or \textbf{shouldn't become data}

            \medskip

            The third kind includes surveillance video, utility and recreation customer records, code complaints that name residents, protective-order addresses, and records about victims and juveniles

            \medskip

            The danger is aggregation. Each record can stay obtainable on its own under CORA, while a searchable index would let anyone assemble a dossier in minutes

            \medskip

            The city has done this before: the published hotline replaces every email address and phone number with a one-way code
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{img/ben-parker.jpg}

            {\scriptsize ``With great power comes great responsibility''}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \note{Taskforce prompts: key risks; mitigation. Guard against being heard as against transparency. Records law doesn't change; what changes is whether the city builds the index. Practical obscurity, the protection of being hard to find, disappears unless someone decides to keep it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Who bears the cost}
    \begin{columns}
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
            What gets scanned gets cited. Records left on paper drop out of every search and analysis, and the least-digitized records tend to describe the least powerful residents

            \medskip

            OCR does worst on old, handwritten, tabular, and non-English pages, so search quality drops along the same lines

            \medskip

            A searchable index exposes residents with the most contact with code enforcement and police far more than anyone else
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/archives-are-incomplete.jpg}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \bigskip

    \begin{alertblock}{Set the order on purpose}
        Let equity decide which holdings are digitized first. The city's own framework already asks it to check data for disparate impact
    \end{alertblock}

    \note{Taskforce prompt: would impacts be disproportionately felt in our community. Don't soften this into a caveat. It is a sequencing rule: it should change which holdings the city digitizes first.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Key takeaways}
    \begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{4pt}
        \item The city holds \textbf{long data}, and most of it a computer can't yet read
        \item Reading pages is cheap now; \textbf{finding and checking} them is the work
        \item The \textbf{hotline} shows the demand: a third of substantive answers were lookups in records the city already holds
        \item \textbf{Accessibility law, CORA, and the city's own framework} already call for this work
        \item Before buying anything: \textbf{an inventory}, stable document IDs and bulk export written into contracts, and a rule that every machine answer cites a page
    \end{enumerate}

    \bigskip

    \begin{alertblock}{A test for the first demonstration}
        Which of the three kinds of holding is its corpus? Does every answer cite a document and page?
    \end{alertblock}

    \note{Taskforce prompt: what would the city need to do. Ten minutes in a browser and two sprints are not an audit. The finding I hold with the most confidence is the one nobody can close yet: the size of the backlog. What the city still holds after a vendor leaves is a choice it can make now, while it is cheap.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile,noframenumbering]{Appendix: reading and checking the totals row}
\begin{lstlisting}
import pdfplumber

def row_words(page, label):
    """Return the words to the right of label, on the line where label is."""
    found = page.search(label)[0]  # where the label sits on the page, in points
    line = page.crop((0, found["top"] - 1, page.width, found["bottom"] + 1))
    return [w["text"] for w in line.extract_words() if w["x0"] > found["x1"]]

for name in REPORTS:
    with pdfplumber.open(name) as pdf:
        page = find_summary_page(pdf)  # the page whose text includes "This report provides"
        earlier, this, change = [to_number(t) for t in row_words(page, "Total Sales and Use Tax")[:3]]
    if this - earlier != change:  # the report's own change column is the check
        print(f"{name}: {this:,} - {earlier:,} is not {change:,}")
# (nothing printed: every change column checks)
\end{lstlisting}

    \small \texttt{to\_number} turns ``\$134,538,892'' or ``(1,145,528)'' into a whole number; accountants write a negative number in parentheses
